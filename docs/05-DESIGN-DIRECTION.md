## 2. Design Decision Summary

The approved design approach is:

> **A modern African climate institution: locally rooted, evidence-led, human, hopeful, and internationally credible.**

The interface should feel:

- serious enough for donors and institutions;
- warm enough for communities and young people;
- confident without becoming corporate;
- contemporary without chasing short-lived trends;
- African in context without decorative stereotypes;
- environmental without becoming a generic green website;
- editorial enough for policy and research;
- practical enough for programme participation and partnership.

The website should not look like:

- a WordPress theme;
- a fundraising template;
- a technology startup;
- a government portal;
- a generic conservation charity;
- a collection of green gradients, leaf icons, and animated counters.

## 3. Design Method

### 3.1 Browser-first design

The project will use a browser-first design process.

A complete high-fidelity Figma design for every page is not required before development begins.

Codex will first build a polished vertical slice containing:

```text
Homepage
→ CPI4CG programme page
→ Related insight or policy brief
→ Get Involved
→ Partnership enquiry form
```

This slice will establish and test:

- colour;
- typography;
- grid;
- spacing;
- image treatment;
- header and navigation;
- programme presentation;
- editorial presentation;
- impact components;
- calls to action;
- form styling;
- responsive behaviour;
- motion;
- accessibility.

After the vertical slice is reviewed, its approved patterns will be reused across the remaining pages.

### 3.2 Why this approach

Browser-first design is selected because:

- the product is content-heavy;
- layout quality depends on real content;
- responsive behaviour must be tested early;
- Sanity content variation must be visible;
- accessibility and performance are part of the design;
- it avoids spending time polishing static screens that do not survive implementation;
- it gives stakeholders a working website to review rather than a picture of one.

### 3.3 Design approval gate

Codex must not mass-produce all page templates before the first vertical slice is approved.

The approval gate should assess:

- brand fit;
- institutional credibility;
- donor readability;
- community representation;
- mobile experience;
- accessibility;
- content density;
- visual hierarchy;
- component reuse.

## 4. Strategic Design Position

SPiD occupies a useful space between:

- grassroots climate action;
- youth leadership;
- community research;
- public policy;
- sustainable development;
- green livelihoods;
- institutional partnership.

The design must communicate all six without allowing one to dominate the others.

### 4.1 Community-rooted

Use real people, places, fieldwork, conversations, data collection, learning, and local environments.

Communities should appear as participants, leaders, researchers, practitioners, and decision-makers—not as passive recipients.

### 4.2 Evidence-led

Use:

- clearly labelled metrics;
- programme milestones;
- research methods;
- publication covers;
- maps only where useful;
- citations or evidence links where approved;
- dates and locations;
- reporting periods.

Evidence should feel integrated into the story, not pasted on as decorative numbers.

### 4.3 Internationally credible

Use:

- disciplined typography;
- consistent spacing;
- clear programme architecture;
- restrained colour;
- excellent photography;
- calm motion;
- reliable forms;
- strong governance and partner presentation;
- publication-quality editorial pages.

### 4.4 Youth-driven

Youth should be visible as:

- fellows;
- facilitators;
- researchers;
- volunteers;
- organisers;
- climate leaders;
- policy contributors.

The site should feel energetic without becoming playful or juvenile.

### 4.5 African in context

African identity should come from:

- real locations;
- people;
- stories;
- environmental textures;
- local language meaning where relevant;
- programme geography;
- photography;
- voice.

Do not rely on generic patterns, map silhouettes, safari imagery, or decorative motifs merely to signal “Africa.”

## 5. Benchmark Principles

The design may learn from leading climate and development organisations without copying their appearance.

### 5.1 Global Center on Adaptation

Useful pattern:

- prominent programme architecture;
- clear Africa-facing adaptation work;
- project and partner relationships;
- visible pathways from strategy to implementation.

SPiD should adopt the clarity, not the scale or visual identity.

### 5.2 World Resources Institute

Useful pattern:

- strong editorial hierarchy;
- one coherent knowledge system;
- evidence, programmes, and insights connected;
- research made readable for broad audiences.

SPiD should adopt disciplined information presentation without reproducing WRI’s institutional density.

### 5.3 ClimateWorks Foundation

Useful pattern:

- funding-facing clarity;
- calm institutional tone;
- strong insights and resource presentation;
- visible strategic focus.

SPiD should use similar confidence while remaining warmer and more community-centred.

### 5.4 African Climate Foundation

Useful pattern:

- African-led climate framing;
- programme priorities connected to finance, capability, and international partnerships;
- confident continental voice.

SPiD should reflect African agency without making claims beyond its present organisational scale.

### 5.5 Benchmark rule

Reference websites provide principles, not templates.

Do not:

- copy their layouts;
- reproduce their colour systems;
- imitate their typography;
- clone their component arrangements;
- make SPiD appear larger or differently structured than it is.

## 6. Brand Personality

The interface should express the following traits.

| Trait | Expression |
|---|---|
| Grounded | Real communities, real environments, practical language |
| Credible | Evidence, dates, sources, restrained design |
| Collaborative | Partner visibility, inclusive imagery, shared action |
| Courageous | Clear headlines, strong calls to action |
| Hopeful | Warm accents, human stories, forward movement |
| Intelligent | Editorial quality, structured research, thoughtful data |
| Transparent | Clear labels, governance, source context |
| African-led | Local agency, regional voice, relevant geography |
| Youth-driven | Energy, contemporary presentation, emerging leaders |
| Responsible | Accessible, privacy-aware, environmentally restrained |

### 6.1 Tone to avoid

Avoid visual tones that feel:

- pitiful;
- alarmist;
- saviour-driven;
- bureaucratic;
- childish;
- trendy for its own sake;
- overly luxurious;
- technologically cold;
- apocalyptic.

## 7. Logo Use

The final SPiD logo file has not yet been audited as part of this document.

Before final brand lock:

- obtain the logo in SVG;
- obtain transparent PNG alternatives;
- confirm official colours;
- confirm minimum clear space;
- confirm whether a horizontal and stacked version exists;
- confirm whether the logo works on light and dark backgrounds;
- create a monochrome version where permitted;
- create a favicon and social avatar.

### 7.1 Temporary build rule

Until the logo audit is complete:

- use the approved available logo;
- do not redraw it;
- do not distort it;
- do not recolour it arbitrarily;
- do not place it over visually noisy imagery;
- use a plain wordmark placeholder only in local development if the real asset is unavailable.

### 7.2 Clear space

Use clear space equal to at least the height of the logo’s primary letterform or symbol around all sides.

### 7.3 Minimum size

The logo must remain legible:

- desktop header: approximately 140–180 CSS pixels wide, depending on proportions;
- mobile header: approximately 112–144 CSS pixels wide;
- favicon: simplified approved mark only.

Exact sizing should be tested with the final asset.

## 8. Colour System

### 8.1 Direction

The colour system should feel environmental without depending on one predictable bright green.

It combines:

- deep forest for trust and climate identity;
- teal for action and participation;
- earth for human warmth and local context;
- sun for emphasis and optimism;
- sand and mist for breathable editorial surfaces;
- dark ink for strong readability.

### 8.2 Provisional palette

The following palette is approved for the first vertical slice, subject to final logo review.

| Token | Hex | Primary use |
|---|---:|---|
| Forest 950 | `#0B2E26` | Dark sections, footer, strong headings |
| Forest 800 | `#145A47` | Primary brand action, links, navigation emphasis |
| Forest 700 | `#176B55` | Hover and secondary brand surfaces |
| Teal 600 | `#0B7662` | Programme accents, active states |
| Leaf 500 | `#4C956C` | Charts, tags, supporting visual accents |
| Earth 600 | `#A84E24` | Warm accent and selected calls to action |
| Sun 400 | `#F2C14E` | Highlights, focus accents, optimistic details |
| Sand 50 | `#F7F3E8` | Warm page background and editorial sections |
| Mist 50 | `#EDF5F1` | Cool supporting surface |
| White | `#FFFFFF` | Primary clean surface |
| Ink | `#13231E` | Main text |
| Slate | `#51615B` | Secondary text |
| Border | `#C8D7D0` | Borders and dividers |
| Error | `#B42318` | Error text and state |
| Success | `#18794E` | Success text and state |
| Warning | `#8A5A00` | Warning text and state |

### 8.3 Approved colour pairings

Use:

- white text on Forest 950;
- white text on Forest 800;
- white text on Forest 700;
- white text on Teal 600;
- white text on Earth 600;
- Ink text on Sun 400;
- Ink text on Sand 50;
- Ink text on Mist 50;
- Ink text on white;
- Slate text only on light surfaces where contrast passes.

Do not use:

- white text on Leaf 500 for normal text;
- white text on Sun 400;
- white text on Earth 500 or lighter;
- Leaf 500 for small text on white;
- pale green text on white;
- colour alone to communicate status.

### 8.4 Colour roles

#### Primary action

```text
Background: Forest 800
Text: White
Hover: Forest 950
Focus: Sun 400 outline plus dark separation
```

#### Secondary action

```text
Background: Transparent or White
Text: Forest 800
Border: Forest 800
Hover background: Mist 50
```

#### Warm action

Earth 600 may be used for one contextual action such as:

- Fund a Programme;
- Support Community Climate Action;
- Sponsor Youth Leaders.

Do not use both primary green and earth actions with equal emphasis in the same component.

#### Links

Standard inline links:

- Forest 800;
- underlined by default within body copy;
- stronger underline or colour change on hover;
- visible focus treatment.

### 8.5 Token implementation

Codex must expose colours through semantic CSS variables or Tailwind theme tokens.

Components should use semantic names such as:

```text
--color-background
--color-surface
--color-surface-muted
--color-text
--color-text-muted
--color-primary
--color-primary-hover
--color-accent
--color-border
--color-focus
--color-error
--color-success
```

Do not scatter raw hexadecimal values throughout component files.

### 8.6 Colour review

Before full rollout:

- compare palette with the final logo;
- test all text and UI contrast;
- test focus visibility on light, dark, image, and coloured surfaces;
- test colour-blind simulations;
- test greyscale understanding;
- confirm print and PDF-download compatibility where relevant.

## 9. Typography

### 9.1 Typeface system

Use no more than two type families.

Recommended:

- **Manrope** for navigation, body copy, labels, buttons, data, and most headings;
- **Newsreader** for selected display headlines, long-form article titles, quotations, and editorial emphasis.

Both should be loaded through the approved Next.js font mechanism and self-hosted by the application build where supported.

### 9.2 Why this pairing

Manrope provides:

- contemporary clarity;
- strong digital readability;
- useful numerical forms;
- confident interface labels;
- a modern youth-facing character.

Newsreader provides:

- editorial credibility;
- warmth;
- distinction for policy and storytelling;
- a human counterbalance to the geometric sans serif.

### 9.3 Typeface restrictions

- Do not use Newsreader for navigation, form labels, buttons, or small text.
- Do not use more than four font weights across the website.
- Do not use all-caps for long labels or headings.
- Do not use letter spacing to make body text appear fashionable.
- Do not use thin font weights.
- Do not place important copy inside images.

### 9.4 Recommended weights

#### Manrope

- 400: body and supporting text;
- 500: navigation and labels;
- 600: subheadings and buttons;
- 700: primary headings and metrics.

#### Newsreader

- 500: editorial display;
- 600: selected article titles or quotations.

### 9.5 Type scale

Use a fluid type scale with `clamp()`.

Recommended semantic scale:

| Token | Mobile | Desktop | Use |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Display XL | 44px | 80px | Homepage hero |
| Display L | 38px | 64px | Major page hero |
| Heading 1 | 36px | 56px | Page title |
| Heading 2 | 30px | 44px | Major section |
| Heading 3 | 25px | 34px | Section group |
| Heading 4 | 21px | 26px | Card or subsection |
| Lead | 20px | 24px | Introductory paragraph |
| Body L | 18px | 20px | Editorial emphasis |
| Body | 16px | 18px | Standard body |
| Body S | 14px | 15px | Metadata and support |
| Label | 14px | 14px | Interface labels |
| Caption | 13px | 14px | Image and figure captions |

### 9.6 Line length

- Standard body: approximately 60–75 characters per line.
- Long-form editorial body: approximately 58–70 characters.
- Hero supporting copy: maximum approximately 45–60 characters per line.
- Do not stretch paragraphs across a full 12-column layout.

### 9.7 Line height

- Display: 0.98–1.08;
- headings: 1.1–1.2;
- body: 1.55–1.7;
- labels: 1.3–1.45.

### 9.8 Heading behaviour

- Use one `h1` per page.
- Do not skip heading levels for visual appearance.
- Keep homepage hero text direct and readable.
- Avoid widow words where practical.
- Do not centre long paragraphs.
- Left alignment is the default.

## 10. Layout and Grid

### 10.1 Container

Use a responsive centred container.

Recommended maximum width:

```text
1280px
```

Allow selected image-led sections to extend wider while preserving readable text columns.

### 10.2 Grid

Desktop:

- 12 columns;
- 24–32px gutters;
- consistent left and right page margins.

Tablet:

- 8 columns;
- 20–24px gutters.

Mobile:

- 4 columns;
- 16–20px side padding.

### 10.3 Content widths

| Content | Recommended maximum |
|---|---:|
| Long-form article | 720px |
| Page introduction | 760px |
| Standard text block | 680–760px |
| Wide data or card area | 1120–1280px |
| Form | 640–720px |
| Image with caption | Depends on role, not arbitrary |

### 10.4 Section spacing

Recommended vertical spacing:

| Context | Mobile | Desktop |
|---|---:|---:|
| Compact section | 40–48px | 56–72px |
| Standard section | 56–72px | 88–112px |
| Major narrative section | 72–88px | 112–144px |
| Card-grid gap | 20–24px | 24–32px |
| Text-stack gap | 16–24px | 20–32px |

### 10.5 Spacing scale

Use a 4px base scale.

Recommended tokens:

```text
4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80, 96, 120, 144
```

Do not use arbitrary one-off spacing unless necessary for optical adjustment.

### 10.6 Alternating surfaces

Use section backgrounds to create rhythm:

- white;
- Sand 50;
- Mist 50;
- Forest 950 for selected strong sections.

Do not alternate background colour mechanically after every section.

Use colour changes to indicate a meaningful change in story or action.

## 11. Shape, Borders, and Depth

### 11.1 Border radii

Recommended:

| Token | Radius | Use |
|---|---:|---|
| Small | 8px | Inputs, tags, compact controls |
| Medium | 16px | Cards and image containers |
| Large | 24px | Feature panels and major media |
| Full | 999px | Tags and compact status pills only |

Do not turn every button, image, card, and section into a pill.

### 11.2 Borders

Use borders to organise content rather than relying on shadows.

Default border:

```text
1px solid Border
```

Stronger dividers may use Forest 800 at low opacity.

### 11.3 Shadows

Use shadows sparingly.

Cards should normally use:

- border;
- surface contrast;
- spacing;
- image hierarchy.

Use a subtle shadow only where elevation communicates behaviour, such as:

- open dropdown;
- floating mobile navigation;
- dialog;
- selected preview control.

Avoid large blurred shadows on every card.

## 12. Photography Direction

### 12.1 Primary rule

Real SPiD photography is the preferred visual language.

Photography should show:

- community meetings;
- field research;
- volunteer data collection;
- climate-learning sessions;
- youth fellows;
- policy dialogue;
- local leadership;
- agriculture;
- water and ecosystems;
- renewable-energy or livelihood activity where relevant;
- collaboration in action;
- real environmental context.

### 12.2 Representation

People should appear:

- active;
- capable;
- respected;
- engaged;
- identifiable where consent allows;
- in context rather than used as decoration.

Avoid:

- pity-based imagery;
- dehumanising close-ups;
- children presented merely as symbols of vulnerability;
- staged cheque presentations;
- generic handshakes;
- “white saviour” composition;
- images that imply partnerships or activities that did not occur;
- stock images presented as SPiD fieldwork.

### 12.3 Climate imagery

Do not overuse:

- hands holding seedlings;
- isolated leaves;
- wind turbines unrelated to the programme;
- solar panels unrelated to the content;
- polar bears;
- melting ice;
- generic disaster imagery;
- globe-in-hands concepts.

Use environmental imagery only when it relates to SPiD’s geography, work, or evidence.

### 12.4 Image ratios

Recommended:

| Use | Ratio |
|---|---|
| Homepage hero | 16:9, 3:2, or editorial split |
| Programme hero | 16:9 or 3:2 |
| Programme card | 4:3 |
| Insight card | 3:2 |
| Team portrait | 4:5 |
| Partner logo | Intrinsic SVG, constrained box |
| Full-width story | 16:9 or 2:1 where composition permits |
| Gallery | Mixed approved ratios with stable layout |

### 12.5 Image treatment

- Preserve natural skin tones.
- Use restrained colour correction.
- Avoid heavy green filters.
- Do not place long text directly over busy images.
- Where text overlays an image, use a tested gradient or separate solid panel.
- Use focal-point cropping through Sanity.
- Keep captions and credits available.
- Avoid excessive rounded masking.

### 12.6 Image metadata

Every editorial image should support:

- alternative text;
- caption;
- credit;
- location;
- date;
- permission or usage status;
- focal point.

### 12.7 Placeholder photography

During development:

- use clearly identified temporary assets;
- never publish unauthorised stock photography;
- never use generated people as documentary evidence;
- replace placeholders before production where the section claims real programme activity.

## 13. Illustration and Graphic Language

Illustration should be secondary to photography and evidence.

Acceptable uses:

- simple process diagrams;
- Theory of Change;
- research methodology;
- programme pathway;
- geographic context;
- climate-system relationships;
- empty states;
- subtle environmental textures.

### 13.1 Style

Illustrations should be:

- simple;
- flat or lightly dimensional;
- geometric;
- restrained;
- compatible with the colour system;
- understandable without decoration.

Avoid:

- cartoon climate mascots;
- glossy 3D characters;
- decorative African patterns without context;
- AI-generated pseudo-documentary scenes;
- complex illustrations that reduce page performance.

### 13.2 Maps

Maps are not required for the MVP.

Where introduced later:

- use only when geography adds meaning;
- include text alternatives;
- avoid heavy interactive GIS for simple location communication;
- use accessible colour and labels;
- provide a list or table alternative.

---

## 14. Iconography

Use one icon family.

Recommended:

- Lucide icons or another approved open-source line-icon set;
- 1.5–2px visual stroke;
- rounded but not playful;
- simple and recognisable.

### 14.1 Icon rules

- Icons support labels; they do not replace important labels.
- Decorative icons use hidden accessibility treatment.
- Functional icons require accessible names.
- Do not mix outlined, filled, and 3D icon families.
- Do not use different icons for the same action.
- Social logos must use official marks.
- Avoid leaf icons as a universal substitute for every climate concept.

## 15. Data and Impact Presentation

### 15.1 Philosophy

Impact data must feel credible before it feels impressive.

Every public metric should provide enough context to understand:

- what was measured;
- where;
- over what period;
- through which programme;
- whether the figure is cumulative or period-specific.

### 15.2 Metric design

An impact metric may contain:

- value;
- unit;
- label;
- short context;
- reporting period;
- geography;
- programme link;
- source note where approved.

Example structure:

```text
368
household responses

CPI4CG pilot
Sabon Gari LGA, Kaduna State
Reporting period: [approved period]
```

### 15.3 Metric styling

- Use large Manrope numerals.
- Use consistent alignment.
- Do not animate values from zero.
- Do not use circular progress charts without a real denominator.
- Do not use decorative charts where a number and sentence communicate better.
- Use charts only when comparison or trend is meaningful.
- Provide text equivalents for charts.

### 15.4 Data colour

Use a restrained sequence based on:

- Forest;
- Teal;
- Leaf;
- Earth;
- Sun.

Do not use red and green alone to communicate positive and negative values.

### 15.5 Claims

The design must not visually overstate small or preliminary findings.

Use accurate labels such as:

- Pilot milestone;
- Expected outcome;
- Current reach;
- Cohort result;
- Cumulative figure;
- Reporting-period figure.

## 16. Header and Navigation Design

### 16.1 Desktop header

The desktop header should feel calm and institutional.

Recommended:

- white or lightly translucent solid surface;
- strong logo presence;
- medium-weight navigation;
- one distinct primary action;
- generous but efficient spacing;
- visible active state;
- no oversized mega-menu.

### 16.2 Sticky behaviour

A sticky header is permitted if:

- it becomes more compact after scrolling;
- it does not obscure keyboard focus;
- it does not cover anchor targets;
- it remains accessible at zoom;
- it does not dominate small screens.

### 16.3 Dropdowns

Dropdowns should:

- use simple grouped links;
- open by click and keyboard;
- have visible focus;
- close predictably;
- use a subtle border and shadow;
- avoid promotional images in the MVP.

### 16.4 Mobile navigation

The mobile menu should be:

- easy to open and close;
- large enough for touch;
- scrollable where necessary;
- hierarchical;
- focus-trapped;
- free from tiny accordion targets;
- visually consistent with desktop navigation.

The primary Partner With Us action should remain visible inside the mobile menu.

## 17. Hero Design

### 17.1 Homepage hero

The homepage hero should make SPiD’s purpose clear within seconds.

Required content:

- headline;
- concise supporting statement;
- primary action;
- secondary action;
- real or approved visual evidence.

Recommended headline:

> **Locally Led. Youth Driven. Climate Resilient.**

### 17.2 Preferred composition

Use one of these approaches:

#### Editorial split

- text on one side;
- strong documentary image on the other;
- warm or neutral background;
- easier accessibility and responsive control.

#### Layered image

- image occupies a large area;
- text sits on a solid or strongly controlled panel;
- no fragile text directly over faces or high-detail areas.

The editorial split is preferred for the first vertical slice.

### 17.3 Hero restrictions

Avoid:

- autoplay video;
- rotating carousel;
- multiple competing headlines;
- more than two primary actions;
- animated word-by-word text;
- giant empty space used only to look premium;
- vague statements such as “Creating a Better Tomorrow” without specific context.

## 18. Card System

Cards are used for:

- programmes;
- insights;
- people;
- partners where appropriate;
- engagement pathways;
- resources.

### 18.1 Card principles

- The whole card may be clickable only when interaction remains accessible.
- Titles must remain visible and readable.
- Cards must tolerate missing optional images.
- Image and text hierarchy must be consistent.
- Tags should be limited.
- Do not overload cards with every CMS field.

### 18.2 Programme card

Include:

- image;
- programme name;
- short summary;
- geography or focus where useful;
- status only where relevant;
- clear link.

### 18.3 Insight card

Include:

- content type;
- title;
- summary or excerpt;
- publication date;
- image where available;
- related programme where useful.

### 18.4 Hover

Hover may:

- change border;
- adjust link underline;
- move the card no more than 2–4px;
- slightly scale the image within a clipped container.

Hover must not be required to understand the card.

## 19. Programme Page Design

Programme pages should balance narrative, evidence, and partnership.

Recommended sequence:

1. Programme hero
2. Overview and quick facts
3. Why the programme matters
4. Approach or methodology
5. Where SPiD is working
6. Activities and milestones
7. Impact or expected outcomes
8. Evidence or resources
9. Partners
10. Related insights
11. Partnership call to action

### 19.1 Quick facts

Use a compact fact panel for:

- status;
- location;
- programme type;
- audience;
- dates where approved;
- thematic focus.

Do not present missing facts as blank labels.

### 19.2 Methodology

CPI4CG methodology can use a clean process or list presentation for:

- household surveys;
- focus group discussions;
- key informant interviews;
- community consultation;
- stakeholder dialogue;
- validation;
- policy engagement.

Avoid an overly decorative infographic.

### 19.3 Partnership action

The final programme call to action should be specific.

Examples:

- Partner on community-led climate governance;
- Support the next AYEDE cohort;
- Collaborate on research and evidence;
- Fund programme expansion.

## 20. Insight and Policy Design

### 20.1 Editorial tone

Insight pages should feel publication-quality.

Use:

- clear title;
- content-type label;
- date;
- author;
- programme relationship;
- strong introduction;
- readable body column;
- figures and captions;
- downloadable resource;
- related content.

### 20.2 Long-form body

- Use Manrope for standard body text.
- Use Newsreader selectively for title, deck, pull quote, or opening emphasis.
- Use generous paragraph spacing.
- Use descriptive subheadings.
- Style lists, quotations, figures, and references consistently.
- Keep share actions secondary to content.

### 20.3 Policy brief presentation

Policy briefs should visibly support:

- cover preview where available;
- executive summary;
- authorship;
- publication date;
- related programme;
- download action;
- file type and size;
- citation information where approved.

The download button must not be the only way to understand the publication.

## 21. Impact Page Design

The Impact page should not be a wall of numbers.

Recommended rhythm:

1. Impact philosophy
2. Selected verified metrics
3. Impact areas
4. Programme-linked evidence
5. One or more stories
6. Publications or learning
7. Partnership action

### 21.1 Evidence hierarchy

Use:

- one to four headline metrics;
- smaller contextual metrics;
- stories;
- methodology notes;
- links to programmes or publications.

Avoid displaying every available number equally.

### 21.2 Impact cards

Impact cards should state:

- metric;
- label;
- period or context;
- programme or location where relevant.

A hover tooltip must not contain essential evidence.

## 22. Team and Advisory Board Design

### 22.1 Team presentation

Use:

- consistent 4:5 portraits;
- name;
- role;
- concise biography;
- approved professional link.

### 22.2 Distinction

Clearly distinguish:

- leadership;
- operational team;
- advisory board.

Do not visually imply that advisers are full-time staff.

### 22.3 Missing portraits

Where an approved portrait is unavailable:

- use a dignified initials or brand-colour placeholder;
- do not use generic stock portraits;
- do not generate a synthetic portrait.

## 23. Partner Design

Partner presentation should build trust without becoming a logo graveyard.

### 23.1 Logo grid

- Use approved logos only.
- Use consistent visual boxes.
- Preserve logo proportions.
- Do not recolour logos unless a monochrome version is approved.
- Provide partner names in accessible text.
- Avoid making one logo appear dominant without reason.

### 23.2 Partnership stories

Where possible, include a short explanation of:

- what the collaboration involved;
- which programme it supported;
- what was produced or learned.

This is stronger than logos alone.

- Fund a Programme
- Sponsor Youth Leaders
- Collaborate on Research
- Volunteer Expertise
- Support Community Climate Action

## 25. Form Design

Forms must feel trustworthy and easy.

### 25.1 Layout

- One column by default.
- Group related fields.
- Keep the form width approximately 640–720px.
- Place instructions before the field they affect.
- Show required status clearly.
- Use visible labels, not placeholder-only labels.

### 25.2 Inputs

Recommended minimum control height:

```text
48px
```

Textarea:

```text
Minimum visible height: 160px
```

### 25.3 States

Every control must support:

- default;
- hover;
- focus;
- completed;
- error;
- disabled.

### 25.4 Validation

- Show inline field errors.
- Provide an error summary for multiple errors.
- Move focus appropriately after failed submission.
- Announce success and failure through accessible status messages.
- Preserve entered data after a recoverable failure.
- Do not use red alone to communicate an error.

### 25.5 Success state

The success state should:

- confirm receipt;
- explain the next step without promising an unapproved response time;
- provide contact email as a fallback;
- offer a useful route back to programmes or insights.

## 26. Buttons and Calls to Action

### 26.1 Button hierarchy

Use three primary styles:

1. Primary filled
2. Secondary outlined
3. Text link with directional indicator

A warm filled action may be used contextually but must not compete with the main primary action.

### 26.2 Button rules

- Minimum height: 44px; preferred 48px.
- Use sentence case.
- Use action-oriented labels.
- Avoid vague “Learn More” where a specific label fits.
- Keep labels short.
- Icons are optional and secondary.
- Do not place more than two prominent buttons in one immediate area.
- Use loading and disabled states for submissions.

### 26.3 Preferred labels

Use:

- Explore Our Work
- Read the Policy Brief
- View Programme
- Partner With Us
- Fund a Programme
- Collaborate on Research
- Contact SPiD
- Download Report

Avoid:

- Click Here
- Submit, where a more specific form action is available
- Discover More, repeated everywhere
- Join the Revolution, unless genuinely appropriate and approved

## 27. Tags, Labels, and Status

Use compact tags for:

- content type;
- programme theme;
- geography;
- publication category;
- programme status when necessary.

### 27.1 Tag rules

- Tags must remain readable.
- Do not use more than three visible tags on a card.
- Do not use colour alone for status.
- Avoid pill overload.
- Do not turn every metadata value into a tag.

### 27.2 Programme status

Possible internal or public status labels:

- Active
- Pilot
- Completed
- Upcoming
- Applications Open
- Applications Closed

Status must come from approved CMS data.

## 28. Motion and Interaction

### 28.1 Motion character

Motion should feel:

- calm;
- responsive;
- intentional;
- restrained.

### 28.2 Duration

Recommended:

| Interaction | Duration |
|---|---:|
| Hover and focus colour | 120–180ms |
| Button or card movement | 160–220ms |
| Dropdown | 160–220ms |
| Mobile menu | 200–280ms |
| Section reveal | 240–400ms, used sparingly |

### 28.3 Easing

Use a small set of shared easing tokens.

Prefer ease-out for entering and ease-in for leaving.

### 28.4 Permitted motion

- subtle card image scale;
- underline movement;
- small arrow movement;
- dropdown fade and translate;
- mobile menu transition;
- gentle first-view section reveal;
- loading indicators.

### 28.5 Prohibited motion

- scroll hijacking;
- parallax backgrounds;
- autoplay carousels;
- rapidly rotating testimonials;
- animated counters from zero;
- constant floating objects;
- looping decorative video;
- text scrambling;
- cursor-following effects;
- flashing elements;
- motion that delays access to content.

### 28.6 Reduced motion

When `prefers-reduced-motion` is enabled:

- remove non-essential movement;
- disable smooth-scrolling animation;
- avoid large transforms;
- keep state changes immediate or nearly immediate.

---

## 29. Responsive Design

### 29.1 Principle

Responsive design is not a desktop layout squeezed onto mobile.

The design should prioritise:

- readable copy;
- touch-friendly actions;
- logical order;
- efficient imagery;
- reduced data cost;
- minimal horizontal movement.

### 29.2 Recommended breakpoints

Use the approved Tailwind breakpoint system with intentional design review around:

- 360px;
- 390px;
- 768px;
- 1024px;
- 1280px;
- 1440px.

Do not design only at exact breakpoint widths.

### 29.3 Mobile behaviour

- Stack hero content.
- Keep primary action visible.
- Convert multi-column grids naturally.
- Avoid horizontal card carousels for essential content.
- Use responsive images.
- Keep navigation and form controls comfortably tappable.
- Preserve meaningful content order.
- Avoid excessive vertical space.

### 29.4 Large screens

Do not allow:

- text lines to become too long;
- cards to stretch awkwardly;
- empty space to dominate;
- imagery to become excessively tall.

---

## 30. Accessibility Design Standard

Target WCAG 2.2 AA.

The design system must support:

- text contrast of at least 4.5:1 for normal text;
- at least 3:1 for qualifying large text;
- at least 3:1 for meaningful UI boundaries and states;
- visible keyboard focus;
- focus that is not obscured by sticky elements;
- touch targets of at least 24×24 CSS pixels under WCAG minimum, with a project preference of 44–48px for primary controls;
- text resizing to 200%;
- reflow without loss of content;
- logical reading order;
- no text embedded in images;
- reduced-motion preferences;
- captions and transcripts for meaningful media;
- meaningful headings and labels.

### 30.1 Focus style

Use a highly visible shared focus treatment.

Recommended:

```text
2–3px Sun 400 outer indicator
plus a contrasting separation line where needed
```

The focus indicator must remain visible on:

- white;
- Sand;
- Mist;
- Forest;
- image backgrounds;
- coloured buttons.

### 30.2 Sticky-header offset

Anchor links and focused elements must not be hidden behind the sticky header.

Use appropriate scroll padding and focus handling.

---

## 31. Environmental Responsibility

A climate website should avoid unnecessary digital waste.

Design choices should support:

- optimised images;
- limited video;
- minimal JavaScript;
- limited font files;
- restrained animation;
- cacheable content;
- no decorative heavy 3D assets;
- no unnecessary background media;
- efficient page length and content hierarchy.

Environmental claims about the website itself should not be made without measurement.

## 34. Empty, Loading, and Error States

### 34.1 Empty state

Empty states should:

- explain why content is unavailable;
- suggest a useful next route;
- avoid implying failure where content simply does not exist.

Example:

> No policy briefs are currently published. Explore our latest programme updates and research insights.

### 34.2 Loading

Public server-rendered pages should minimise visible loading states.

Where loading is necessary:

- preserve layout dimensions;
- avoid large spinners;
- use restrained skeletons;
- respect reduced motion.

### 34.3 Error state

Errors should:

- use plain language;
- avoid technical codes as the only explanation;
- provide a retry or alternative route;
- offer contact information where useful;
- never expose stack traces.

### 34.4 Not found

The 404 page should:

- clearly state that the page was not found;
- provide links to Home, Our Work, Insights, and Contact;
- maintain the SPiD visual identity;
- avoid jokes that reduce institutional trust.

## 36. Component Styling Rules

All components must:

- support light and dark surfaces where specified;
- work with long CMS titles;
- work without optional images;
- work with one or many records;
- preserve heading hierarchy;
- provide keyboard focus;
- avoid layout shift;
- use semantic HTML;
- respect reduced motion;
- remain readable at zoom;
- avoid hard-coded copy.

Component variants must be purposeful.

Do not create a variant merely to solve a one-page spacing problem.

## 37. Design Anti-Patterns

Codex must avoid:

- generic green gradient heroes;
- rotating homepage carousels;
- autoplay video;
- animated metric counters;
- glassmorphism;
- oversized shadowed cards;
- excessive pill shapes;
- decorative blobs behind every section;
- stock images pretending to be programme evidence;
- text over unreadable photography;
- long centred paragraphs;
- icon-only navigation;
- tiny grey text;
- invisible focus;
- generic “Learn More” on every card;
- inconsistent button styles;
- excessive entrance animations;
- a logo strip with distorted partner marks;
- unverified quotes;
- decorative charts without meaningful data;
- dark mode in the MVP unless separately approved;
- copying a benchmark organisation’s design.

## 38. Content and Design Relationship

Design must adapt to content rather than forcing content into fixed marketing lengths.

### 38.1 Content priority

When content and decoration conflict:

1. accuracy;
2. comprehension;
3. accessibility;
4. action;
5. visual polish.

### 38.2 Missing content

If content is incomplete:

- omit the optional section;
- use an approved neutral empty state;
- keep the CMS record in draft;
- do not generate fake copy to protect the layout.

### 38.3 Long content

Long programme and policy content should use:

- section navigation where useful;
- strong headings;
- summaries;
- side facts;
- pull quotes only when approved;
- related resources.

Do not shrink text or compress spacing to make content fit a predetermined screen.

- Are impact figures contextualised?
- Are programme statuses accurate?

- Are spacing and alignment consistent?
- Are images purposeful?
- Are cards and colours restrained?

- Is keyboard focus visible?

## 45. Design Definition of Done

A design milestone is complete only when:

- the implementation uses approved tokens;
- real content has been tested;
- mobile and desktop views have been reviewed;
- accessibility checks pass;
- focus and form states are complete;
- image behaviour is stable;
- loading, empty, error, and missing-content states exist;
- motion respects user preference;
- no unapproved placeholder is publicly exposed;
- performance remains within the agreed budget;
- stakeholder feedback is recorded and resolved;
- new design decisions are added to the project decision log.
